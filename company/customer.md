# Who Allternit sells to

## Ideal customer profile (as evidenced, not aspirational)

**Reference customer:** Swyft Market, Inc. (Delaware C-corp) — an early-stage startup with an existing mobile app and website "of unknown completion state," needing a codebase audit plus ongoing website/build work. Billed hourly (T&M, Net-15) after a small fixed-fee Phase 1 audit.

**Pattern that implies:** small businesses and early-stage startups that (a) already have *some* digital presence or codebase, often unfinished or inherited, (b) don't have in-house engineering depth to trust their own assessment of it, and (c) need someone to both audit reality and then execute the fix — not just hand over a report.

**Secondary, catalog-evidenced segment:** solo operators / local businesses / freelancers buying discrete, well-scoped deliverables off the fixed-price catalog (one-page sites, resume rewrites, spreadsheet cleanup, SOP documentation) — lower trust required, shorter sales cycle, Tier A "publish now" work.

**Tertiary (per business.md's Build/Retrofit/Run/Husks lines):** SMBs and operations-heavy businesses (warehouse, packaging, light manufacturing) evaluating AI automation or robotics retrofits — earlier-stage relationship, audit-first, longer cycle.

## Pains (what actually shows up)

- An existing website, app, or codebase in unclear condition — inherited, half-built, or undocumented — and no reliable way to assess it internally.
- Manual, repetitive back-office work (data cleanup, inbox triage, CRM hygiene, reporting) eating founder/staff time.
- Knowing "AI could probably help" but not knowing which parts of the business, or how to scope it without getting oversold.
- Wanting execution, not just a strategy deck — the gap between "we assessed the problem" and "it's actually fixed."

## Objections and fears (read directly off the catalog's own guardrail language — these are the concerns Allternit itself preemptively addresses)

- **"Will this actually work, or is it an AI gimmick?"** → addressed by "never promise production from an untested single prompt," audit-first policy on anything advanced/regulated.
- **"Will you overreach into what I didn't ask for?"** → addressed by mandatory change-order process, phased scope with acceptance criteria per deliverable.
- **"Can I trust you with my data/credentials?"** → addressed by NDA-before-discovery-if-data-comes-up, "keep account actions supervised," "never accept contracts, move money, impersonate the client."
- **"Are you going to guarantee results you can't back up?"** → explicit refusal to guarantee rankings, sales, ad results, or reviews anywhere in the catalog.
- **Regulated/compliance fear** (health, finance, legal-adjacent data) → Tier C classification, audit-first, human-approval-before-production language throughout.

## Buying triggers

- A website/app/system that's stalled, inherited, or unclear in scope (the Swyft pattern) — usually surfaces as "we need someone to look under the hood before we can even scope this."
- A specific, nameable bottleneck (inbox overload, manual data entry, no analytics, slow site) matched directly against a Tier A catalog line — fast close, low trust bar.
- Growth past the point where manual ops scale — CRM/reporting/automation requests cluster here.

## Decision criteria (as encoded in the kickoff playbook itself)

1. Does the request match a known catalog line? If yes — quote from the starter range directly (fast, low-friction).
2. Does it touch integrations, migrations, production automation, or regulated data? If yes — audit first, never quote blind (slower, trust-building phase required).
3. Red flags that get a request declined or re-scoped: demands for guaranteed rankings/results, credential access requested without a signed agreement, regulated data without compliance clarity.

## Language customers use (from the actual intake form and SOW)

Customers describe things in terms of "company overview," "primary bottleneck," "technical environment," "AI/RAG needs," "privacy/compliance," and "budget & timeline" — the intake form's own section order is itself a signal for how customers frame their ask (business context first, technical detail second, budget last).
